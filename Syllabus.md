**Course Description:**

"Data in History" asks four core questions: What counts as data? Why do people trust numbers? How has that trust shifted over time? And how can we use data \- including AI-assisted methods \- to study the past? This course combines hands-on learning in coding, historical thinking, and AI-assisted analysis with big historical datasets, and considers how numeric thinking has come to organize modern life. Using "big-data" approaches to the history of African slavery in the Americas — drawing on sources like ship manifests, censuses, and plantation records — we'll examine what these sources reveal and conceal about the past, while weighing the ethical and methodological limits of working with such evidence, including the new possibilities and risks that AI tools bring to historical analysis.

By the end of this class, hopefully you will have developed a marketable set of skills to harness data and the critical awareness to do so ethically.

This course is a hands-on, experimental, learn-as-we-go experience that introduces both students and the faculty to the use of digital tools and sources to conduct original historical research, formulate historical arguments, and communicate historical ideas in digital formats (audio, visual, textual). 

**Learning Goals \-**

* **Coding** \- learn the rudiments of coding, particularly how process, analyze, and visualize data using the Python programming language  
* **AI Assisted Coding** \- learn how to use LLMs ([Claude.ai](http://Claude.ai)) to supercharge your ability to wrangle, analyze, and visualize data  
* **History** \- understand data as a historical artifact (or a historical construction) and its role within different historical topics. To explore how humanities understand data and knowledge creation.  
* **Data Literacy** \- evaluate data within its larger cultural, social, and political contexts.

**About the course:**

**How this course is designed \-**  
In the name of full transparency, this class is an experiment.  I am a historian, and I am expert in the history of slavery in Spanish America specifically. I am growing as a digital historian (you’ll get to see some of my work later in the semester), and I have experienced how the advent of LLMs like Claude and ChatGPT have accelerated my ability to do that work. I am not a computer scientist, or a data analyst, and I can GUARANTEE that many (if not most) of you are much more knowledgeable than I in this regard. So, I will be learning alongside you. This class is a hybrid \- unlike a traditional coding course, you’ll be studying data through a historical lens; unlike a traditional history class, we’ll be learning the basics of code and to use LLMs to create data driven components to make historical arguments.  

I have designed this class with three interrelated and interwoven tracks:

**Track 1 \- History, Data, and Slavery**  
The goals of this track are perhaps two fold.  First, we want to come to understand how historians and economic historians (these are not the same) have deployed data (big and small) to better understand the institution of African slavery in the United States.  Second, this will allow us to develop a historical and theoretical framework for understanding just what “data” are. A central theme of the course is that “all data have a history.” Through our coursework, we will learn about the historical dimensions of data: how, when, by whom, and why certain types of data have been collected. The goal is to build our capacity to decide when and how data should or shouldn’t be used.

**Track 2 \- Programming in Python**  
The course will teach you the very basics of using the Python programming language to work with data. Again, the goal here is two fold. 1\) Algorithmic thinking and Failure; and 2\) to prepare you for using AI to do advanced data analysis for your final project. In order to do AI-assisted coding, analysis, and visualization well, you have to understand how code works. It will help you write better prompts to an AI and it will allow you to assess how and why an LLM made an error.

**Track 3 \- Digital Humanities in the Age of AI**  
One of the more exciting realities of the Age of AI is how it lowers the entry threshold to doing advanced digital humanities work. With a little coding, some understanding of the dangers of AI, and some understanding of how LLMs work, we can really accelerate our ability to work with data and create meaningful visualizations.

NOTE: In the end, all of the types of analyses, data wrangling, and visualizations we will play with during the semester can be done in a coding environment. If you are uncomfortable using LLMs for any reason, and prefer to work in a traditional coding environment that is totally acceptable.

**Grading-**   
In the name of experimentation, I am not going to be grading in the traditional sense in this class. I worry that students are too focused on “grades”; and less focused on the process of learning and maybe even mastering new things. My primary goal as an educator is to help you *learn how to learn\!* A key component of that process is to learn how to persevere through failure. For some of you, the code will be easy. For others, thinking historically will be.  You will help me assess the areas for growth and improvement for you as a thinker and interpreter of data. In addition, “grading” digital history work can be difficult.  Traditional grading practices focus on the “product,” but the process and the learning through failure along the way are the key elements of digital humanities learning.  So, we’re gonna lean into that.

Each of you is starting from a different place in terms of your expertise AND in what you might hope to get out of this semester.  Regardless of your starting point, we want to play in a space that values experimentation, creativity, and failure. So, you are going to articulate what you want to learn in this course, and self-assess your own growth.

Thus, I will not be using a **traditional letter grading model**. Rather, I will rely on a system of consultative grading in which you take ownership over your own learning. 

Here is how this will work functionally. You will submit various types of assignments throughout the semester.  I will provide **qualitative feedback** on those submissions \- I will indicate what you did well and identify areas for improvement. You can look for codes \- if i say it’s “ok,” or “good,” or “excellent”, that will give you some indication of the general quality of the work from my perspective.

Throughout the semester you will be asked to submit a series of **three self-reflective assignments**. In each, you will reflect on your own learning goals and how well you feel you are accomplishing them. You will also indicate what you feel you are accomplishing “behind the scenes” \- helping classmates, taking intellectual risks, going above and beyond the expectations of an assignment, etc.  In your final self-assessment, you will assign yourself a grade for the semester and explain your reasoning behind it (and “because I want it” is not a good reason).  I reserve the right to adjust this grade up or down if I think you are being too hard on yourself or are taking advantage of the opportunity. \[This will, of course, be shaped by my perspective on your work throughout the semester.  And, it will also take into account the peer assessment of your final project as well\].

**Learning Community \-**  
This course will work best as a shared experiment rather than a lecture \- you're not just learning to code, read critically, or think like a historian, you're building toward what AI-assisted historical work can and can't do, and everyone in the room will get there together, including me. That means your fumbles, half-working scripts, and half-formed interpretations are part of the material, not embarrassments to hide — the "fail log" habit we'll build into our assignments is as much about normalizing that as it is about debugging. I'll ask you to be generous with classmates (and faculty) who are earlier in their coding journey than you, with classmates for whom thinking historically feels alien, and with yourself when the AI gets something wrong that you didn't catch — noticing that, and asking why, is exactly the habit of mind a historian brings to any source, data included.

**Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI)**

Generative AI tools (like ChatGPT and Claude) are really good at writing code (and are getting much better at writing history). In fact, you could use an LLM to help you write all of the code for this course and to summarize and assess all of the assigned readings in much less time that it will take you to do that work on your own. But, I am going to ask you not to.

Why are you in college? Why are you at Denison in particular?  Even if you think your answer is “to get a job,” what is it that we are supposed to offer you to achieve those goals?  Think about your potential answers to these questions.

My answers are these \- to learn how to learn; to learn how to apply current knowledge to new problems; to learn how to teach ourselves new skills; to practice failing productively; to build resilience; to be comfortable with ambiguity and ideas that challenge our own.  The list could go on and on.

The course is structured to encourage you to think computationally and to think historically at the same time. Computational or algorithmic thinking includes breaking complex problems into smaller tasks, recognizing patterns, and designing step-by-step solutions. These are the real skills that make someone effective at working with data, and they can only be developed through hands-on practice. 

This course will take a sliding approach to AI, in the beginning of the semester I will ask that you refrain from using AI at all.  Towards the end of the semester, we will turn our attention to using GenAI tools to do analysis and visualization. And, for the final project, you will have free reign to use AI tools (or not) to help you write and debug code to create the best product you can.

**Reading Discussion Boards \-** 

Rather than passively reading on your own, we will use Discussing Boards in Canvas to deepen our engagement with the things we will be reading.  For many class periods, pre-selected students will be required to initiate the conversation, and the rest of the class will be expected to engage those questions and ideas, and or present questions and ideas of their own.

I will be using Claude to help me assess the quality of these discussion posts.  Here is a general rubric:

## 

| Tier | Description |
| ----- | ----- |
| **Checking the box** | Restates the reading in your own words. No specific passage cited. No real question, or a purely rhetorical one ("Isn't it interesting that..."). Could have been written without doing the reading closely. |
| **Circling it** | Engages a specific point from the reading, but stays descriptive rather than analytical. May ask a question, but one the reading already answers, or one too broad to actually discuss ("What do you all think about slavery and data?"). |
| **Digging in** | Raises a genuine question — one you don't already know the answer to. Connects the reading to something beyond itself (another reading, a contradiction within the text, a current event, a prior week's argument). Engages a specific detail, quote, or data point rather than the reading in general. |
| **Carrying it forward** | Does everything in "Digging in," and also advances the class's collective thinking — responds to a classmate's post specifically, complicates or pushes back on an argument (the author's or a classmate's), or surfaces a tension the class hasn't already discussed. |

**Core Assessments**

**Slavery, History, and Data Paper \-**  
*Across this semester, historians have turned enslaved people into data — prices, valuations, headcounts, coordinates on a map — and explored how historical actors in the past turned enslaved people into data. And historians continue to debate the ethics in doing so.  Your job, in paper of 1,250 words or fewer, is to reflect on these conversations.  How has this work shifted (if at all) your thinking about the meaning and value of data? Of historical argumentation?*

**Project Review \-**  
In teams of two (2), you will write a review of a data-driven historical project.  It can focus on an academic publication (book chapter or article), a piece of long-form journalism, a website, or any other substantive work that focuses on a historical topic and uses data (or computational analyses) in its analysis or presentation. You will pick the project, subject to my approval, and will be expected to do a 7-8 minute in-class presentation for your classmates.

**Data Bibliography \-**  
You will write a data bibliography on an assigned historical dataset. You will be responsible for familiarizing yourself with the data, the history that surrounds it, and the historical arguments it might be used to address.

**Research Project \-**  
The major culminating assignment for this course is a research project in which you find or create a dataset that allows you to investigate a historical topic. You will have to conduct research on that topic to explore how other historians (or people who think historically) have addressed the general topic.  You will then use Python or AI-generate code to process, analyze, and visualize the data to present an argument, interpretation, or narrative based on that analysis.
